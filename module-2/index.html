01 / The idea

Why I wanted to make a little ballet

I have been dancing for 18 years, so ballet felt like something I could bring to this project personally. I wanted to give someone who does not dance a small way into that world. They would not need to know choreography or learn a sequence themselves. They could make a few choices and watch a little ballerina perform their dance.

I called it Your Turn because the visitor gets to decide what happens on stage. I wanted it to feel cute and easy to approach. The result is a four-step dance made with a joystick, a knob, and a button.

02 / The interaction

One control for each decision

The joystick moves a spotlight to the next position on the stage. The potentiometer selects a plié, turn, or jump. Pressing the separate yellow button saves that movement and position, and the dancer previews it. After four choices, she performs the complete sequence.

Keeping those decisions separate was important to me. Someone should be able to understand the project after a short explanation. The movement names appear on screen, the chosen movement is highlighted, and four boxes show the steps already added. The preview also lets the visitor see what their choice means before the full dance.

The small LED from the lab circuit lights when the yellow button is held. It gives another visible sign that the press is being registered. The ESP32 screen is not used here: the stage runs on the laptop, which also powers the board through USB.

03 / The first build

Soldering was the part I was nervous about

“I was worried that I had already made the board unusable.”

As a CS major, I was much more familiar with software than with building a physical circuit. I had never soldered before. One header looked straight and fully seated, but the other did not line up as neatly and had a pin sticking out a little. I was worried that I had already made the board unusable.

I followed the demonstration video from class and used a multimeter in continuity mode, with its sound enabled. Touching the probes to the two points I was checking produced a beep, which told me there was an electrical connection between them. That was reassuring, but it did not prove that the whole circuit worked. The next confirmation came from testing the button, joystick, and knob with the software. I kept the same ESP32 and breadboard.

I built up the tests gradually. First the button could turn on the light, then I could move the joystick, and eventually I could turn the potentiometer and see its value change. Seeing a physical action turn into something on the laptop was probably the most satisfying part. By the end, I felt much more confident that I could build and troubleshoot hardware myself.

04 / Reading the controls

Getting the board and laptop to understand each other

The ESP32 reads the controls; Processing turns those readings into a dance. I started from our Lab 2 controller examples.

01 · Getting the readings right +

The problem

The LED worked, but the old visualizer expected three values. The board was sending five.

if(values.length!=5) continue;

The receiver now checks for all five values before using a reading.

At first, turning the knob did not change the reading as expected. I had connected its output to pin 13 while the test was set up for pin 27; once the code matched my wiring, the knob responded.

05 / Saving a dance

Remembering choices and turning them into a dance

The tricky part was keeping choosing, previewing, and playback separate. Each button press needed to count only once.

02 · One press, one step +

The challenge

Keep new choices from interrupting a preview.

if(phase!=0 || count>=4) return;
stepMove[count]=selected;

Accept a choice only when ready, then save it. Four choices become the dance.

06 / Small revisions

Making the ballerina and revising the stage

My professor suggested looking for premade sprites. The version I ended up using draws the ballerina directly in Processing: lines make the limbs, ellipses make the head and bun, and a triangle and ellipse make the tutu. There are no downloaded dancer images.

03 · The spotlight, before & after +

I noticed the light stayed behind when the dancer moved.

Before · fixed to my marker

ellipse(markerX,markerY,140,40);

After · follows her during playback

float lightX = phase >= 2 ? dancerX : markerX;
float lightY = phase >= 2 ? dancerY : markerY;
ellipse(lightX,lightY,140,40);

I still choose the spot with the joystick. During playback, the light moves with her.

I followed my professor's suggestion to keep restarting simple: close and rerun the sketch for the next visitor. I also requested full-screen scaling so the stage would be larger when presenting.

07 / In class

Letting other people try it

“The second I explained it, they knew how to use it.”

In class, I got a lot of positive feedback. After I explained the controls, people knew how to use them and enjoyed making their own sequence. They also thought the ballerina was cute, which was exactly the feeling I wanted.

For me, that ease of use mattered as much as the animation. I did not want visitors to spend their time figuring out a complicated interface. I wanted them to make a few choices and see a dance that belonged to them.

The biggest change for me was my confidence with the physical part. I started out worried about the soldering, and finished with a controller I had assembled and tested myself. Watching the software respond to that circuit made the project feel much more tangible than something that lived only on my laptop.
