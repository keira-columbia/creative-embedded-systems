01 / The idea

Why I wanted to make a little ballet

I have been dancing for 18 years, so ballet felt like something I could bring to this project personally. I wanted to give someone who does not dance a small way into that world. They would not need to know choreography or learn a sequence themselves. They could make a few choices and watch a little ballerina perform their dance.

I called it Your Turn because the visitor gets to decide what happens on stage. I wanted it to feel cute and easy to approach. The result is a four-step dance made with a joystick, a knob, and a button.

02 / The interaction

One control for each decision

The joystick moves a spotlight to the next position on the stage. The potentiometer selects a plié, turn, or jump. Pressing the separate yellow button saves that movement and position, and the dancer previews it. After four choices, she performs the complete sequence.

Keeping those decisions separate was important to me. Someone should be able to understand the project after a short explanation. The movement names appear on screen, the chosen movement is highlighted, and four boxes show the steps already added. The preview also lets the visitor see what their choice means before the full dance.

The small LED from the lab circuit lights when the yellow button is held. It gives another visible sign that the press is being registered. The ESP32 screen is not used here: the stage runs on the laptop, which also powers the board through USB.

03 / The first build

Soldering was the part I was nervous about

“I was worried that I had already made the board unusable.”

As a CS major, I was much more familiar with software than with building a physical circuit. I had never soldered before. One header looked straight and fully seated, but the other did not line up as neatly and had a pin sticking out a little. I was worried that I had already made the board unusable.

I followed the demonstration video from class and used a multimeter in continuity mode, with its sound enabled. Touching the probes to the two points I was checking produced a beep, which told me there was an electrical connection between them. That was reassuring, but it did not prove that the whole circuit worked. The next confirmation came from testing the button, joystick, and knob with the software. I kept the same ESP32 and breadboard.

I built up the tests gradually. First the button could turn on the light, then I could move the joystick, and eventually I could turn the potentiometer and see its value change. Seeing a physical action turn into something on the laptop was probably the most satisfying part. By the end, I felt much more confident that I could build and troubleshoot hardware myself.

04 / Reading the controls

Getting the board and laptop to understand each other

There are two programs. The Arduino sketch reads the controls on the ESP32. The Processing sketch receives their values and turns them into movement on the stage. The controller code builds on our Lab 2 examples. The final project combines the controller with a custom Processing program.

Open the code notebook +
Serial.printf("%d,%d,%d,%d,%d\n", x, y, joystickSwitch, knob, button);

This line sends five numbers, separated by commas, followed by a newline: joystick X, joystick Y, the joystick's own click switch, the knob, and the separate button. The newline tells Processing where one complete reading ends. Both programs use 9600 baud, and the board sends a reading roughly every 50 milliseconds.

One confusing moment was having the button work while the old joystick display seemed stuck. The old visualizer expected three values, while the combined controller sent five. The laptop program needed to be updated too. A working LED alone could not tell me that the laptop was interpreting the data correctly.

if(values.length!=5) continue;
stickX=n[0]; stickY=n[1];
knob=firstPacket ? n[3] : lerp(knob,n[3],0.25);

Processing waits for a complete line and checks its length before using it. The array positions have to match the order sent by the board. The last line takes the first knob reading directly, then gradually blends later readings into it. This reduces the small fluctuations I saw when the knob was sitting still.

The potentiometer was another wiring-and-code lesson. We had initially been discussing pin 27, but I connected its output to pin 13. The code was changed to const int potentiometerPin = 13; to match the actual circuit, and the test then worked. For someone rebuilding it, the final pin assignment matters more than the wire color.

The controls also need a little tolerance. A small dead zone keeps tiny joystick variations from moving the spotlight. The knob has a margin around each movement boundary so it does not keep switching between two choices. The button must stay stable briefly before a press is accepted, so one press does not become several steps.

05 / Saving a dance

Remembering choices and turning them into a dance

The most involved part of the software was separating choosing a step, previewing it, and playing the whole sequence. The same button should not keep adding steps while the ballerina is already performing a preview.

Open the code notebook +
if(phase!=0 || count>=4) return;
stepX[count]=markerX; stepY[count]=markerY;
stepMove[count]=selected;
count++; phase=1; beginAnimation();

The first line only allows a new step while the program is in its choosing phase, and stops it from storing more than four. The next lines save the spotlight's coordinates and the selected movement in three arrays. The count increases, and phase 1 starts the preview. This is how a temporary choice becomes part of the choreography.

The program has four phases: choosing, previewing, playback, and finished. After the fourth preview, it goes through the stored steps in order. The code uses elapsed time rather than stopping the program with a long delay, so drawing and reading the controller can continue during the animation.

float eased = travel*travel*(3-2*travel);
dancerX = lerp(fromX, stepX[i], eased);

The dancer travels from her previous position to the saved position for the current step. lerp calculates a point between those two places. The first line makes her start and finish that travel more gently instead of moving at one abrupt constant speed.

06 / Small revisions

Making the ballerina and revising the stage

My professor suggested looking for premade sprites. The version I ended up using draws the ballerina directly in Processing: lines make the limbs, ellipses make the head and bun, and a triangle and ellipse make the tutu. There are no downloaded dancer images.

Open the code notebook +
float wave=sin(PI*t);
float jump=move==2 ? 80*wave : 0;

As the movement progresses, t goes from 0 to 1. The sine value rises from zero and returns to zero, so the jump lifts the dancer and brings her back down. The plié uses that same rise-and-fall pattern to bend the legs. The turn changes her horizontal width and flips the drawing to suggest rotation. These are simplified gestures rather than realistic ballet animation.

After trying the full sequence, I noticed that the spotlight stayed at the last position I had selected instead of following her. I asked for it to follow during playback. The earlier drawing used markerX and markerY throughout; the updated version chooses between the marker and the dancer:

float lightX = phase >= 2 ? dancerX : markerX;
float lightY = phase >= 2 ? dancerY : markerY;

While choosing steps, the light belongs to the visitor's joystick. During playback and at the end, it belongs to the dancer. I also requested a larger presentation view; the current sketch scales the original stage to fit the full screen without stretching her proportions.

I followed my professor's suggestion to simplify restarting. Instead of adding another reset control, the finished dance stays on screen. Closing and rerunning the Processing sketch clears it for the next visitor.

07 / In class

Letting other people try it

“The second I explained it, they knew how to use it.”

In class, I got a lot of positive feedback. After I explained the controls, people knew how to use them and enjoyed making their own sequence. They also thought the ballerina was cute, which was exactly the feeling I wanted.

For me, that ease of use mattered as much as the animation. I did not want visitors to spend their time figuring out a complicated interface. I wanted them to make a few choices and see a dance that belonged to them.

The biggest change for me was my confidence with the physical part. I started out worried about the soldering, and finished with a controller I had assembled and tested myself. Watching the software respond to that circuit made the project feel much more tangible than something that lived only on my laptop.
